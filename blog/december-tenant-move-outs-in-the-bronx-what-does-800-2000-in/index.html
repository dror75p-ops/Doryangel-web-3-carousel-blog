December Tenant Move-Outs in the Bronx: What Does $800–$2,000 in Turnover Inspection Actually Catch—and Why Skipping It Costs You $3,000+ in Damage Claims?

A proper December turnover inspection runs $800–$2,000 in the Bronx and catches slow leaks, failing boilers, and window drafts before they snowball into $3,000+ repair bills and HPD violations after your new tenant moves in.

A proper December turnover inspection in the Bronx runs $800–$2,000 depending on unit size and building age, and it typically catches three expensive things before they bite you: a slow plumbing leak under a sink, a boiler that won't hold 68°F through January, and window caulk that's about to let in cold air and mold complaints. Skip it, and the same issues show up six weeks later as a $3,000+ repair bill, a Class B or C HPD violation, and a security-deposit dispute you're going to lose in housing court because you never documented the unit's condition on move-out day.

If you've ever stood in an empty apartment on December 28th with keys in your hand, a new tenant arriving January 1st, and no idea whether the radiators actually work — you're exactly who this post is for. Let's walk through what that inspection money is really buying you.

Why Is a December Turnover Different From Any Other Month?

In our experience managing Bronx walk-ups from Mott Haven up through Fordham, December turnovers carry two risks that July turnovers simply don't:

  • The heat-season clock is already running. NYC Housing Maintenance Code §27-2029 requires 68°F minimum daytime indoor temperature from October 1 through May 31. If your new tenant moves in January 1st and the boiler sputters out January 3rd, you have hours — not days — before an HPD heat complaint lands. A single Class B heat violation runs $350–$500, and HPD forces expedited repairs on your schedule, not theirs.
  • Vendor rates spike after January 1. The HVAC companies our clients use quote roughly a 40% emergency markup on boiler calls once the first real cold snap hits. A $500 tune-up in mid-November becomes a $700 panic call in January — for the same work.

That's why the inspection has to happen before the new lease starts, not after the first complaint. For the longer-range picture on how fall and winter turnovers compare across the river, our Bronx vs. Mount Vernon fall turnover breakdown walks through the full cost spread.

What Does the $800–$2,000 Actually Buy You?

Here's roughly what our vendors have been quoting in the Bronx for a full turnover inspection on a 1–2 bedroom apartment:

  • Licensed plumber walk-through: $150–$300 for supply-side diagnostics, plus $85–$125/hour if anything needs opening up. They're checking under-sink shut-offs, toilet tank fill valves, and the tub drain for slow leaks.
  • HVAC diagnostic + boiler tune-up (if building-wide): $150–$250 diagnostic, $400–$600 for a full cleaning, inspection, and efficiency test. Takes 2–3 hours.
  • Window and weatherstripping refresh: $400–$800 for 8–10 windows, 4–6 hours of one tradesperson's time.
  • General contractor punch-list walk: $200–$400 to document wall damage, flooring, appliance function, and smoke/CO detector status with photos and dates.

Add it up and most 1–2 bedroom Bronx units land between $800 and $1,400. A larger 3-bedroom in an older pre-war building can push $2,000 once you factor in more windows and a longer plumbing check.

What Does the Plumber Actually Look For?

This is the line item owners most want to cut — and the one that costs them the most when they do. Here's what a slow leak looks like before it becomes a disaster:

  • Under-sink cabinets: The plumber runs the faucet for 2–3 minutes while feeling the underside of the cabinet. A slight spongy give in the cabinet toe-kick means water has been soaking the particleboard for weeks.
  • Shut-off valves: White, crusty mineral deposits around the little oval handles under the sink or behind the toilet mean the valve is weeping — slowly enough you'd never see a puddle, fast enough to rot the floor underneath over a winter.
  • Toilet base: A rocking toilet or discolored caulk ring at the floor means the wax seal is failing. That leak goes straight into your downstairs neighbor's ceiling.

A slow drip under 1 drop per second often runs 4–8 weeks before anyone sees it, and the subfloor repair alone runs $1,200–$3,500. None of this is DIY — NYC requires a licensed plumber for any work on fixtures, drains, or supply lines. Your job as the owner is to notice the warning signs and call the right trade. How Should You Walk the Boiler Room Yourself?

Even if you're hiring a vendor, we tell owners to do a 10-minute boiler-room look-see the day after the tenant leaves. You're not fixing anything — you're deciding whether the vendor needs to come this week or next month.

Look for three things:

  1. The low-water cutoff (the safety switch, usually a boxy device mounted on the side of the boiler, that shuts everything down if the water level drops too low). If there's rust streaking down from it or a red light blinking, flag it for the vendor.
  2. The sight glass — a small vertical tube on steam boilers showing the water level. It should be about half full and the water should look clear-ish, not coffee-colored.
  3. The pressure gauge. On a residential steam system you want to see 0–2 psi at rest. Anything higher and something's off.

None of this is a repair. It's just you being able to tell the vendor "the sight glass is brown and the pressure gauge is pegged at 5" instead of "the heat is weird." That one sentence cuts a diagnostic visit in half.

Can You Caulk the Windows Yourself?

Yes — this one genuinely is owner-doable, and it's where a lot of the mold complaints come from in Bronx pre-war buildings. When caulk shrinks, peels, or cracks, cold air sneaks in, warm interior air hits the cold glass, and you get condensation that grows mold within a few weeks. Tenants then file a Class C HPD complaint ($250–$350) and you're paying a remediation vendor instead of a $12 tube of silicone.

For an owner comfortable with a ladder and a caulk gun, a tube of exterior-grade silicone, a utility knife to strip the old bead, and 10 minutes per window handles it. If the sashes themselves are rotting or the glass is loose in the frame, that's a window-contractor job — don't try to re-glaze old pre-war sashes yourself.

What Should You Document Before Handing Over Keys?

The inspection is only half the value. The other half is the paper trail that protects your security deposit position if the outgoing tenant disputes damages. Before the new lease starts, make sure you have:

  • Dated photos of every room, every appliance interior, and any existing wear.
  • Meter readings (gas, electric, water if sub-metered) written down with the date.
  • Written vendor reports from the plumber and HVAC tech, with their license numbers.
  • A signed move-out condition sheet from the outgoing tenant, or documented proof you offered a joint walk-through and they declined.

If you're keeping any part of the deposit, NYC law requires an itemized statement within 14 days — and "the place was dirty" doesn't hold up. Dated photos and licensed-vendor invoices do. Our full NYC Landlord Compliance Guide covers the deposit timing rules in detail.

The Math, Plainly

Spend $800–$2,000 in December, and you walk into January with a documented unit, a working boiler, sealed windows, and no slow leak quietly eating your subfloor. Skip it, and the realistic downside stack looks like this: $1,500 subfloor repair + $450 HPD heat violation + $300 mold remediation + $700 January emergency boiler markup = $2,950 before you've even dealt with the deposit dispute.

The inspection isn't the expense. The inspection is what keeps the expense from happening.
