Bronx vs. Yonkers: Why Your Summer Tenant Turnover Could Cost 30% More (And What Property Managers Know)

Summer turnover in the Bronx runs roughly 30% higher than in Yonkers once you count stricter NYC notice rules, lead paint remediation, and a tighter re-leasing window. Here's the real math and what to do about it.

If you own a rental on both sides of the city line — or you're weighing a Bronx building against one in Yonkers — the turnover math is not the same. In our experience managing Bronx buildings since 2010, a summer vacancy on the Bronx side typically runs about 30% more expensive than the same unit would in Yonkers, driven mostly by NYC-specific notice rules, lead paint compliance, and a compressed re-leasing calendar. Below is what actually drives that gap and how to shrink it before June hits.

Why the Bronx Costs More to Turn Over

Yonkers is close enough that owners assume the rules travel across the Bronx River. They don't. Yonkers falls under New York State's Real Property Law — the 30-day notice under RPL §226-c is generally sufficient, and Good Cause Eviction only applies where the city has opted in with its own thresholds. The Bronx, by contrast, is under the full stack of NYC rules: HPD, Local Law 7 lead paint, and the 2024 Good Cause Eviction Law (Local Law 73) that covers a large slice of market-rate units.

Those extra layers add real days and real dollars to every move-out.

How Much Are We Actually Talking About?

Here's a same-unit comparison we walk clients through — a two-bedroom renting for $1,600, turning over August 1:

Cost line Bronx Yonkers
Vacancy loss (avg. days empty) 45–60 days ($2,400–$3,200) 30–40 days ($1,600–$2,100)
Lead paint (Local Law 7) remediation $400–$1,500 on pre-1978 stock Not required at re-lease

Typical total $3,650–$6,400 $2,250–$4,400

That's the roughly 30% premium in one table. On a small portfolio it adds up quickly — three summer turnovers on the Bronx side and you've quietly given back a month of gross rent.

The Three Rules That Drive the Gap

What Does NYC Notice Actually Require?

Under NYC's implementation of RPL §226-c and HPD guidance, a tenant who has lived in the unit more than one year but less than two needs 60 days' written notice of non-renewal or a rent increase over 5%. Over two years, it's 90 days. Yonkers follows the state baseline, which many owners still run at 30 days for shorter tenancies.

In plain terms: if you want the unit empty August 1 in the Bronx, you often needed to send certified notice by early May. Miss that window and the tenant has a right to stay another full cycle — and your "summer turnover" becomes a February turnover in the worst leasing month of the year.

Lead Paint: The Silent Bronx Tax

NYC Local Law 31 (and the older Local Law 1/Local Law 7 framework) requires an XRF lead inspection at every turnover in any pre-1960 building where a child under six could reside. Most Bronx walk-ups qualify. Yonkers has no equivalent turnover trigger.

Here's what that actually looks like in the field: a licensed EPA RRP contractor comes in with a handheld XRF gun (it reads lead through paint layers without scraping), tests door frames, window sashes, and baseboards, and gives you a certified report. If anything reads hot, that surface has to be either encapsulated (sealed with a special coating) or fully remediated before the new tenant moves in. The inspection itself runs $300–$500 in the Bronx; remediation on a single window frame can be another $400–$800. HPD violations for skipping this run $265–$3,500 each, and they attach to the building.

This is licensed work — not a DIY item, ever. But you can control the timing. Book the XRF inspection the week the outgoing tenant signs their notice, not the week they hand back keys.

Good Cause Eviction Compresses Your Options

Local Law 73 (2024) means many Bronx market-rate tenants now have a presumptive right to renew unless the rent increase stays under the CPI-plus-5% threshold (roughly 8.5% for 2026). That doesn't exist in Yonkers unless the city opts in. Practical effect: Bronx owners have less leverage to price-reset a unit at renewal, which pushes more turnover into the summer window and drives up demand for vendors right when they're most expensive.

What Bronx Property Managers Actually Do Differently

After a decade of summer turnovers, the pattern that works is boring and calendar-driven:

April: Pull every lease ending June–September. Calculate the exact notice deadline for each (30, 60, or 90 days depending on tenancy length). Send certified mail. This one step eliminates most of the vacancy-loss overrun.

May: Schedule the XRF lead inspection and pre-book your painter for the last week of the tenant's lease. Bronx painters book up by mid-June; if you call in July you're paying rush rates or waiting three weeks.

June: Start marketing 30 days before move-out. In hot pockets like Mott Haven, Riverdale, and Fordham, five to seven showings usually produces a qualified applicant in 10–14 days. Screen carefully — the rules are getting stricter every year, and our 2026 Bronx tenant screening guide walks through what's still legal to ask.

July: Walk the unit with the outgoing tenant. This is the single best protection for the security deposit — a signed condition report, dated photos of every room, and a written punch list. The security deposit rules under the HSTPA are unforgiving if you don't itemize within 14 days.

August: New tenant in. Lead paint certificate on file. Window guards installed if there's a child under 10. HPD registration updated.

The Yonkers Owner's Blind Spot

One last thing worth naming: owners who scale from Yonkers into the Bronx routinely underestimate this gap in year one. They price rents based on Yonkers turnover math, hit their first August vacancy, and discover the real number is $1,500–$2,000 higher per unit. If you're modeling a Bronx acquisition, use the Bronx column of that table above — not the Yonkers one you're used to.
