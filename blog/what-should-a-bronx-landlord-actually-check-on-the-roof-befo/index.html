What Should a Bronx Landlord Actually Check on the Roof Before Fall Hits?

A plain-English walkthrough of what to look at on your Bronx roof in August, what a real inspection costs, and which parts you can eyeball yourself versus what belongs to a licensed roofer.

If you're standing at the bottom of your stoop wondering whether the roof will make it through another Bronx winter, you're asking the right question at the right time. An August roof check on a typical Bronx building means a $150–$300 visit from a local roofer (or a careful DIY look from the hatch), and it catches the small stuff — pooling water, alligatored tar, loose flashing — before the first October nor'easter turns a $500 patch into a $12,000 replacement. That's the whole point of doing this in August instead of after the leak shows up on your top-floor tenant's ceiling.

We've walked a lot of Grand Concourse, Norwood, and Throggs Neck roofs over the years, and the pattern is almost always the same: the owners who look in August pay for small repairs, and the owners who wait until leaves are falling pay emergency rates. Here's how to actually do the check.

What Your Roof Is Made Of (and Why It Matters in August)

Most older Bronx rowhouses and small apartment buildings have what's called a built-up roof — layers of tar and gravel laid down flat, sometimes called a "tar and gravel" or BUR roof. Newer buildings and recent replacements often have a modified bitumen or EPDM rubber membrane instead (a big rolled-out sheet, usually black or white, glued or torched down).

All three types fail from the same two enemies: UV sun damage and thermal cycling — the daily expand-and-contract from Bronx summer heat hitting 95° and then cooling off at night. By August, your roof has been baking since May. That's exactly when the damage becomes visible if you know what to look for.

What You Can Safely Check Yourself

A visual look from the roof hatch, a fire escape landing, or the edge of a ladder is squarely owner territory. You do not need a license to look. What you should never do yourself: walk across a roof you're not sure of, patch anything with hot tar, or touch any structural work on a building over six stories — that's Local Law and DOB territory and you'll want a licensed roofer with the right filings.

Here's what an owner can actually eyeball in about 20 minutes on a dry August morning:

  • Standing water in the valleys or low spots. After a rain, water should drain within 48 hours. If you see puddles that never leave, the roof is sagging or the drains are clogged. Standing water is the number-one killer of flat roofs in the Bronx.
  • Alligatoring. This is the trade word for that crosshatch pattern of little cracks in the tar surface — it literally looks like alligator skin. It means the top layer has dried out from UV and is about to let water through. Small patches are fine to note; if it covers more than a quarter of the roof, that's a full re-coat conversation.
  • Granule loss. Walk your gutters and downspouts. If you're finding piles of what looks like coarse black sand, that's the protective mineral surface washing off your roof membrane. A little is normal. A lot means the membrane is aging out.
  • Soft or spongy spots. If a section feels like walking on a mattress, the insulation underneath is wet. Don't stand on it — mark it and call a pro.
  • Flashing around chimneys, parapets, and the roof hatch. Flashing is the metal (usually aluminum or copper) that seals the seams where the roof meets a wall or a pipe. Lifted, rusted, or separated flashing is where 80% of Bronx roof leaks actually start — not in the middle of the roof.
  • Parapet walls. On rowhouses and small apartment buildings, the short brick wall around the roof edge is the parapet. Look for cracked mortar, loose bricks, or the coping stones on top shifting. This ties directly into the exterior checks you should be doing every spring too.

Write down what you see. Photos with your phone are perfect. You want a record for the roofer and for your insurance file — insurance carriers are getting stricter about documented maintenance this year, and photos of a clean August inspection are exactly the kind of thing that keeps your premium from jumping.

What an August Roofer Visit Actually Costs

Here's roughly what we see Bronx roofers charge in 2026 for straightforward small-building work. Prices climb for larger buildings, taller access, and anything requiring scaffolding.

Job Typical Bronx cost Time on site
Basic visual inspection + written report $150–$300 30–45 min
Localized patch (20–50 sq ft) $400–$800 2–4 hours
Re-coating a built-up roof (sealer + top layer) $2,500–$5,000 1–2 days
Full BUR replacement, 2,000–2,500 sq ft rowhouse $8,000–$15,000 3–5 days

The seasonal math is the part most owners miss. From October through December, Bronx roofers are booked solid, and emergency call-outs after the first heavy rain can add 50–100% to labor and 15–25% to full replacement quotes. The same $200 inspection you skipped in August becomes a $400 emergency visit plus whatever the leak did to your top-floor unit's ceiling and your tenant's patience.

When to Call a Licensed Roofer (Not a Handyman)

A few situations are non-negotiable — please don't let a general handyman touch these:

  • Any building over six stories — NYC requires licensed roofing work and often a DOB filing.
  • Anything involving the structural deck underneath the membrane (rotted wood, sagging).
  • A full replacement on any building — you want the manufacturer's warranty, and that only comes with a licensed installer.
  • Any work that touches a shared parapet wall with a neighboring building. That's a party-wall issue and you want it done right and documented.

For a simple patch on a two- or three-family rowhouse, a reputable local roofer without a full GC license is usually fine — but ask for proof of insurance and get the scope in writing. The compliance side of this (filings, Local Law inspection cycles, DOB paperwork) is worth skimming in the NYC landlord compliance guide so you know when a permit is actually required.

What to Ask the Roofer When They're On the Roof With You

Go up with them if you can. Five questions to ask, in plain language:

  1. "How many years do you think this roof has left as-is?"
  2. "If we do nothing this year, what's the most likely thing to fail first?"
  3. "Is this a patch situation or a re-coat situation?"
  4. "What's your warranty on the repair, in writing?"
  5. "Are you licensed and insured, and can I have a copy of the certificate?"

Write the answers down. Keep them in the building file with the photos. Next August, you'll compare notes and see whether anything got worse — that's how you catch a slow failure before it becomes a claim.
