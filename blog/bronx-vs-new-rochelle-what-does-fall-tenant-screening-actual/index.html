Bronx vs. New Rochelle: What Does Fall Tenant Screening Actually Cost—and Why Skipping It Costs You $5,000+ in Problem Tenants?

Proper fall tenant screening runs about $20 per applicant in the Bronx and $35–$50 in New Rochelle — skipping it can mean $5,000+ in lost rent, legal fees, and 90–180 days stuck in Bronx Housing Court.

If you have a vacancy to fill before Thanksgiving and you're tempted to just hand keys to the first person with cash in hand — we get it. Bronx landlords lose roughly $75–$125 in rent for every week an apartment sits empty, and that math gets loud in October. But in our experience managing Bronx buildings since 2010, the tenants placed in a rush between Halloween and New Year's are the ones who show up in Housing Court by March.

Here's the short answer: proper fall tenant screening costs about $20 per applicant in the Bronx (capped by state law) and roughly $35–$50 per applicant in New Rochelle (no cap). Skipping it or doing it sloppily routinely costs Bronx owners $5,000 or more in a single bad placement once you add lost rent, filing fees, attorney time, and turnover.

What Does Screening Actually Cost on Each Side of the City Line?

The single biggest difference between screening in the Bronx and screening in New Rochelle is a 2019 state law almost no small landlord knows about.

New York's Tenant Screening Practices Law (effective October 2019) limits what you can charge an applicant to the actual cost of the background and credit report, and never more than $20 total. Violate it and General Business Law §527 lets the AG hit you for up to $1,000 per violation. That law applies everywhere in New York State — including New Rochelle — but in practice Westchester landlords and their brokers still charge $35–$50 because enforcement has been loudest in the five boroughs.

So your real cost per applicant looks like this:

Line item Bronx New Rochelle
Max fee you can charge applicant $20 $20 (enforced lightly)
Typical credit + eviction report wholesale $18–$22 $18–$22
Criminal background check $10–$15 $10–$15
Income/employment verification (your time or vendor) $0–$25 $0–$25
Your net out-of-pocket per applicant $8–$25 $8–$25

Call it $25 per applicant, all in. Screen three applicants on a vacant unit and you're at $75. That is the entire upfront cost of doing this right.

Why Skipping It Costs $5,000+ — the Bronx Math

When screening fails, the bill lands in four places at once. Here is what we have actually watched happen on Grand Concourse and in Mott Haven:

  • Lost rent while the tenant stops paying. The average Bronx nonpayment case takes 90–180 days from petition to marshal removal. On a $1,800/month apartment that's $5,400–$10,800 in rent you will almost certainly never recover.
  • Housing Court costs. Filing fees, service of process, and an L&T attorney in Bronx Civil Court run $300–$800 on an uncontested case, and $1,500–$3,500 if the tenant answers and the case gets adjourned (they almost always do).
  • Good Cause complications. Under the Good Cause Eviction Law (effective February 2024), you now have to prove good cause for nonpayment in covered units. If your file doesn't show you verified income at move-in, tenant's counsel will argue the rent was never affordable in the first place. We've seen this add 60 days and $2,000+ in legal time.
  • Turnover and re-rental. After eviction you're looking at paint, locks, often appliance replacement, and another vacancy month. Budget $2,500–$4,000 even on a clean turnover.

Add it up on a typical Bronx two-bedroom and one skipped screening easily becomes an $8,000–$15,000 event. The $25 report would have caught it.

New Rochelle landlords face a shorter timeline — Westchester Housing Court moves cases in roughly 60–120 days — but higher per-month rent ($2,400–$3,200 is common) so the lost-rent number is similar.

What Fall Screening Should Actually Look Like

One of the most common questions we get this time of year is "what do I actually run on an applicant?" Here's the file we keep on every placement, and it's the same one that holds up in Housing Court:

  1. Government ID — verify in person or over video, not just a photo.
  2. Credit + eviction report from a tenant-screening vendor (TransUnion SmartMove, RentPrep, or similar). Look for prior Housing Court filings in the name — this is the single strongest predictor.
  3. Income verification at 40× monthly rent. NYC's standard. For a $1,800/month Bronx unit, that's $72,000/year gross. Pull two recent pay stubs, most recent W-2, and if self-employed, two years of tax returns. If you skip this on a $1,500/month apartment, you are quietly accepting $60,000+ in annual rent liability without evidence they can cover it.
  4. Landlord references — call the landlord before the current one. The current one may want them gone.
  5. Criminal background check — allowed in NYC, but the Fair Chance for Housing Act (effective January 2025) limits when and how you can consider it. Document your process.
  6. Written, consistent screening criteria applied to every applicant. Fair Housing Act Section 109 violations carry $16,000+ in statutory damages plus attorney fees. The protection is simple: same rules, same order, every time, in writing.

Why Fall Specifically?

The applicant pool shifts between October and January. Strong tenants with good credit tend to move in spring and summer when school and job cycles align. The fall and early-winter pool skews toward people who had to move — job loss, breakup, prior eviction, lease nonrenewal. That is not a reason to be unfair to anyone; it is a reason to actually run the reports instead of trusting a good first impression.

We've also seen the same seasonal pattern on vacancy costs — the owners who rush fall placements to avoid a vacant December often end up with a vacant April and a court case. There's more on that tradeoff in this Bronx vs. Mount Vernon comparison on fall turnover costs.

The Honest Comparison

Bronx landlords have a legal ceiling that protects them from over-charging applicants but also means screening feels "cheap" — so it gets skipped or outsourced to whoever is cheapest. New Rochelle landlords charge more and often screen more thoroughly because the fee covers it. The Bronx owners who consistently outperform are the ones who treat the $20 cap as the price of admission to a 2-year tenancy, not as a reason to cut corners.

One screened applicant costs you $25. One unscreened applicant costs you a winter, a spring, and a summer in Bronx Housing Court. That's the whole comparison.
