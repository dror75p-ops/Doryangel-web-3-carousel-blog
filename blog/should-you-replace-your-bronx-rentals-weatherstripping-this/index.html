Should You Replace Your Bronx Rental's Weatherstripping This Fall, or Just Caulk Around the Frames? What $300–$1,200 Actually Saves You

If your Bronx tenant is already complaining about drafts, you need to know which fix the window actually needs — a $300 tube-of-caulk job you can do yourself, or an $800–$1,200 weatherstripping replacement. Here's how to tell them apart before the heat season starts.

If a tenant has already texted you about a cold draft by the window, here's the short answer: caulking seals the gap between the window frame and the wall, and weatherstripping seals the gap between the moving sash and the frame itself. They are not interchangeable. In most Bronx apartments the right fall move is caulking first (roughly $300–$500 or a weekend of your own time), and full weatherstripping replacement ($800–$1,200 per unit) only when the gaskets are visibly cracked, flattened, or letting daylight through. Doing the wrong one costs you the same money twice and still leaves you out of compliance when the November HPD heat complaints start rolling in.

We've walked a lot of pre-winter window inspections across Mott Haven, Fordham, and Riverdale, and the pattern is almost always the same — owners either over-spend on a full replacement they didn't need, or they smear caulk over worn-out gaskets and wonder why the apartment is still 64°F in January.

First, What Are These Two Things, Actually?

Before you spend a dollar, it helps to know what you're looking at when you stand in front of the window.

  • Caulking is the flexible sealant (usually a polyurethane or acrylic-latex paste from a tube) that fills the stationary joint where the window frame meets the plaster or brick of the wall. Think of it as grout — once it's in, nothing moves. It fails by shrinking, cracking, or pulling away from one side of the joint.
  • Weatherstripping is the rubber, foam, or silicone gasket seated in a channel along the parts of the window that actually open and close — the bottom of the lower sash, the top of the upper sash, and the sides where the sash slides. It has to stay springy to seal, because it gets compressed every time the window shuts. It fails when the material hardens, flattens, or tears.

If cold air is coming in along the edge of the wall around the frame, that's a caulk problem. If cold air is coming in where the window itself meets the frame when closed, that's a weatherstripping problem. Shine a flashlight from outside at night and have someone inside look for the glow — wherever light leaks through, air is leaking too.

The Honest Cost Breakdown for a Bronx 2–3 Bedroom

Here's what we actually see owners pay in the Bronx right now:

Fix Typical cost per unit Who does it Lifespan
Caulk re-do around frames (DIY) $40–$80 in materials You, one Saturday 5–7 years
Caulk re-do (hired handyman) $300–$500 Vendor, 2–3 hrs + $75–$150 call-out 5–7 years
Full weatherstripping replacement $800–$1,200 Licensed carpenter/window pro 10–15 years
Both, done together on an older unit $1,000–$1,500 Pro 10+ years

A typical 2–3 bedroom apartment needs 80–120 linear feet of weatherstripping once you count all the windows plus the entry door and any basement or common-area doors. That's why the quote jumps so much — it's not the material cost, it's the labor of pulling old gaskets, cleaning the channel, and seating new ones so the sash still closes cleanly.

How to Tell Which One Your Window Actually Needs

Stand inside the apartment on a sunny afternoon with the window closed and locked. Then:

  1. Look for daylight around the frame. A hairline of light along the perimeter where the frame meets the wall = caulk is shrunk or cracked. That's a caulk job.
  2. Run your hand along the sash edges (top, bottom, and where it meets the sill). Feel for a cool draft or press the gasket with your fingernail. If it's hard like a plastic ruler instead of soft like a pencil eraser, it's dead. That's weatherstripping.
  3. Try the dollar-bill test. Close the window on a dollar bill and pull. If it slides out with almost no resistance, the gasket has lost its compression — replace it.
  4. Check the corners of the sill for black streaks or soft wood. That means water has been getting in for a while, and you may be past a simple caulk fix into actual sill repair. Call a carpenter before you spend on anything else.

If one or two windows fail the test and the rest look fine, don't let anyone sell you a whole-apartment weatherstripping replacement. Spot-fix those windows and re-caulk the rest.

What You Can Do Yourself vs. What to Hire Out

Caulking is genuinely owner-doable. You need a $4 caulk gun, a $7 tube of good exterior-grade polyurethane sealant (not the cheap painter's caulk — it won't survive a Bronx freeze-thaw cycle), a utility knife to cut out the old bead, and a damp rag. The job on one window is about 20 minutes once you've done one. No permit, no license, no DOB involvement.

Full weatherstripping replacement is where we tell owners to stop and hire out — especially if the window is a double-hung with weight pockets, or if the sash has to come out of the frame. Improper installation can void the window manufacturer's warranty and, more painfully, cause water intrusion that shows up as a stain on the downstairs tenant's ceiling six months later. A licensed carpenter or window specialist in the Bronx charges $75–$150 just to show up, but they'll do it right and give you a line-item receipt you can file.

If your building was built before 1960 and you're disturbing painted surfaces, you're in lead-safe work practice territory, which pushes the caulking job alone from $300 up toward $500 because the vendor has to use containment and HEPA cleanup. Don't skip this — it's one of the easier things HPD and EPA cite on.

Why This Matters Before November

Heat season in NYC runs October 1 through May 31, and you're legally required to keep the apartment at 68°F during the day and 62°F overnight (see our NYC Landlord Compliance Guide for the exact citations). A window that leaks like a sieve forces your boiler to run harder, drives up your fuel bill, and still leaves the tenant cold enough to call 311. One substantiated HPD heat violation can run $250–$500 per day of non-compliance, which eats a $1,200 weatherstripping job in a week.

Document everything. Take dated photos of the gasket condition and the caulk line before and after. Keep the receipt. If a tenant later files a heat complaint, those photos are your defense that you addressed drafts proactively. While you're already at the window on a cold-weather walkthrough, it's worth bleeding the radiators in the same visit — a $50 parts job that often fixes the "my apartment is cold" complaint even when the windows are fine.

A Simple Rule of Thumb

If you're walking into fall and you're not sure where to start: caulk every unit, replace weatherstripping only where it fails the dollar-bill test. That gets most Bronx owners to a tight, compliant apartment for well under $500 per unit. Save the $1,200 replacement for the units that genuinely need it, or for the Riverdale and south-facing exposures where UV has cooked the gaskets flat.
