Bronx vs. Mount Vernon: Which Landlords Actually Pay Less to Replace Fall-Damaged Gutters Before Winter Clogs Them?

A side-by-side look at what gutter replacement really costs on a Bronx multi-family versus a Mount Vernon two-family this fall — vendor fees, per-foot pricing, which parts you can safely do yourself, and when you have to call a licensed roofer.

If you've been staring at a sagging gutter run on your Grand Concourse walk-up wondering whether it's cheaper to deal with it now or let it ride into January — Bronx owners generally pay more per foot than Mount Vernon owners, but the gap is smaller than most landlords think, and the real money is lost by waiting until ice forms behind a bad seam and soaks the top-floor ceiling. On a typical 150-foot perimeter, you're looking at roughly $1,500–$2,800 installed in the Bronx versus $1,200–$2,200 in Mount Vernon for seamless aluminum. The rest of this post walks you through why, what you can do yourself before calling anyone, and which parts you absolutely should not touch.

Why the Bronx Costs More (and Why It's Not That Much More)

In our experience booking fall gutter work for Bronx owners since 2010, three things push NYC pricing up: parking, insurance, and the walk-up itself. A Mount Vernon crew pulls into a driveway at a two-family on Oak Street and sets ladders in the yard. A Bronx crew blocks a lane on Morris Avenue, pays a flagger, and hauls 30-foot sections up and over a parapet wall.

Here's the honest side-by-side we see this fall:

Line item Bronx multi-family Mount Vernon 1–2 family
Call-out / assessment fee $75–$150 $0–$75
Labor rate $65–$85/hr $45–$65/hr
Seamless aluminum, installed $10–$15/linear ft $8–$12/linear ft
Downspout replacement $85–$140 each $65–$110 each
Fascia board repair (per 8-ft section) $120–$220 $90–$160
Typical 150-ft job, total $1,500–$2,800 $1,200–$2,200

A few hundred dollars sounds like a lot until you price out a Class C water-infiltration violation under NYC Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2056, which requires gutters and leaders (that's the trade word for downspouts — the vertical pipes that carry water from the gutter down to the ground) to be kept watertight. One ice-dam leak into a tenant's bedroom in February will cost you more in drywall, paint, and HPD paperwork than the entire Bronx-vs-Mount-Vernon price gap.

What a Failing Gutter Actually Looks Like

Most owners don't walk their own roofline until something drips. Here's what to look for from the sidewalk with a cup of coffee, before you ever climb a ladder:

  • Rust streaks running down the fascia (the flat wood board the gutter is nailed to). That means water has been sitting in the gutter for weeks instead of draining.
  • A visible dip or sag in a long run — gutters are supposed to slope about a quarter inch for every 10 feet toward the downspout. If you see a belly, the hangers underneath are pulling loose.
  • Staining on the brick or siding directly under a seam. Seams are where two sections of gutter meet; they're sealed with a rubbery caulk that dries out and splits after about 7–10 Bronx winters.
  • A downspout that wiggles when you push it. The straps have rusted through and the whole pipe is about to come off in a nor'easter.
  • Water pooling at the foundation after a rain. The downspout is dumping right next to the wall instead of carrying water away, which rots the sill and invites basement leaks.

If you see two or more of these on the same run, that run is coming down this winter whether you plan for it or not.

What You Can Safely Do Yourself

For a 2–4 family in the Bronx with a reachable one- or two-story roofline, there's real money to save by doing the maintenance side yourself and only paying a pro for the replacement. We encourage owners to handle:

  • Cleaning the gutters. Two to four hours with a sturdy extension ladder, work gloves, a plastic scoop, and a garden hose. Scoop the leaves into a bucket, flush with the hose from the far end toward the downspout, and watch where the water goes.
  • Re-sealing a split seam. A $9 tube of gutter sealant (polyurethane, not silicone — silicone won't stick to aluminum) and a caulk gun. Dry the seam, run a bead on the inside, smooth it with a gloved finger.
  • Re-strapping a loose downspout. New aluminum straps are about $3 each at any Bronx hardware store on Fordham Road. Two screws into the mortar joint, not the brick itself.
  • Testing drainage. Run a hose into the gutter at the end farthest from the downspout for five minutes. Water should flow steadily out the bottom with no overflow at any seam.

Do this work in September or early October. Our fall roof and gutter cost comparison covers the broader timing, but the short version: cleaning and small repairs by October 15 keeps you out of ice-dam season.

Where You Have to Call a Licensed Pro

Stop and hire out the moment any of these are true:

  • The roofline is three stories or higher, or you'd need to stand on the roof itself.
  • The fascia board is soft or spongy — that's rotted wood backing, and new gutters nailed into rot will fall off by March.
  • The gutter ties into a flat roof membrane or a parapet scupper (the opening cut through a parapet wall that drains the roof). Mess with that and you void the roof warranty.
  • You're doing a full tear-off and replacement of more than one run.

Full replacement in NYC should go to a roofing contractor or Licensed Master Plumber when framing is involved. No DOB permit is required for routine gutter work on its own, but anything that ties into a roof replacement does. What to Ask the Contractor Before You Sign

Three questions separate the honest Bronx gutter guys from the ones who'll be in Florida by Christmas:

  1. "Are you using .032-gauge aluminum or thinner .027?" The thicker gauge costs about $1 more per foot and lasts twice as long in freeze-thaw.
  2. "How many hangers per 10 feet, and are they hidden hangers screwed into the fascia?" Answer should be at least one every 24 inches, screwed — not the old spike-and-ferrule nails that pop out.
  3. "Will you test the slope with water before you leave?" If they won't run a hose through it in front of you, don't pay the final draw.

Get the final invoice itemized, take photos of every new run and sealed seam, and file them with your lease paperwork. If HPD ever shows up on a water complaint, those photos and the dated receipt are what close the case.

So Who Actually Pays Less?

Per linear foot, Mount Vernon wins by roughly 20–25%. But Bronx owners who clean and reseal in September — before calling anyone — routinely spend less total for the season than Mount Vernon owners who skip the maintenance and end up replacing two runs plus a rotted fascia in December. The borough on your tax bill matters less than the week in October you put a ladder up.
