Why Your Bronx Building's Windows Need a Summer Inspection—And What Water Damage Costs You Before Fall

Walk your windows in June, July, or August, look for cracked caulk and cloudy glass, and re-seal while the weather is dry — it's the difference between a $15 tube of silicone and a $3,000 rotted frame come November.

If you own a Bronx walk-up, your windows are quietly the most expensive thing on the building you're not looking at. Here's the short version: walk every window between June and August, check the caulk and the glass for the specific failure signs below, re-seal what you can with exterior silicone while the weather is dry, and call a glazier for anything structural. Do that one Saturday now and you almost certainly avoid the $3,000 rotted-frame-and-mold repair we watch owners pay every spring.

We've been managing Bronx buildings since 2010, and window water damage is one of the top three things we see cost owners real money — right behind roof leaks and boiler failures. The frustrating part is how preventable it is.

Why Does Summer Matter So Much for Windows?

Caulk and sealant are the whole game. The bead of silicone or polyurethane running around your window frame is what keeps rain out of the wall cavity. UV, summer heat, and winter freeze-thaw slowly break it down until it shrinks, cracks, or pulls away from the brick or wood.

Most exterior caulks need 48–72 hours of dry weather to cure properly. That's the whole reason summer is the only sensible time to touch this. Re-caulk in October and the first cold rain washes half of it out of the joint before it sets. Water then sits inside the frame all winter, and by March you've got rot, mold behind the plaster, and a stain on the top-floor tenant's ceiling.

Think of caulk like the grout in a shower: same idea, same failure mode, same consequences when you ignore it.

What Are You Actually Looking For on a Window Walk-Through?

Grab a flashlight, a notebook, and thirty seconds per window. You're checking five things:

  • The caulk line around the outside of the frame. Run your fingernail along it. If it's shrunk away from the brick, cracked, chalky, or missing chunks, it's done.
  • Condensation between the two panes of glass. That cloudy or foggy look inside the sealed unit means the double-pane seal has failed. You can't fix that with caulk — the glass unit itself has to be replaced.
  • Drafts with the window shut. Hold the back of your hand around the frame on a breezy day. Air movement means water movement too.
  • The interior sill and the wall just below it. Water stains, bubbled paint, or soft drywall means you already have a leak. That's not a summer maintenance job anymore — that's a repair.
  • The sash balance (the spring or cord that holds a double-hung window open). If the window slams shut on its own or won't stay up, a handyman fix runs $40–$90 per window and is worth doing before a tenant props it with a book and cracks the glass.

Write down the apartment number and which window on each finding. You'll forget by dinner otherwise.

What Can You Fix Yourself, and What Needs a Pro?

Here's the honest split, because this matters for your wallet:

Do it yourself:

  • Re-caulking a frame perimeter. Cut out the old caulk with a putty knife, wipe the joint clean, and lay a fresh bead of exterior-grade silicone ($8–$15 a tube at any Bronx hardware store). One tube does 2–3 windows. Budget an hour per window the first time.
  • Tightening loose hardware, replacing a torn weatherstrip, swapping a sash balance.

Call a pro:

  • Any fogging between panes — that's a glass unit swap, $300–$800 per window installed by a licensed glazier (the trade name for a window glass specialist). A handyman call-out to diagnose runs $75–$150.
  • Rot in the wood frame itself. Once water's been sitting in there, the wood is spongy and won't hold new caulk. That's a carpenter job.
  • Full window replacement across the building. A 20-unit walk-up runs $8,000–$15,000, and if you're changing the opening size or the structural frame you'll need a DOB Alteration Type 2 permit. Don't let anyone talk you into skipping that — unpermitted window work shows up in due diligence and kills sale prices, which is a real issue when you're looking at what your building is worth heading into fall.

A reasonable rule: if it involves caulk, weatherstripping, or a screw, it's yours. If it involves glass, framing, or a permit, it's not.

The HPD Angle

Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2013 requires every window in a rental unit to be "watertight and weathertight." A tenant with a drafty, leaking window can file an HPD complaint, and inspectors regularly write these up as Class B violations — the "hazardous" tier, with certification deadlines and per-day penalty exposure if you miss them.

We've seen one persistent leaking window in a top-floor unit generate three separate violations (the window itself, the water-damaged wall, and the resulting mold) from a single inspection. Fixing it in July for a tube of caulk vs. fixing it in February under an HPD order is not the same job. The full picture of what inspectors look at lives in our NYC landlord compliance guide.

What Does Skipping a Summer Actually Cost?

Here's the math we watch play out every year:

  • Summer re-caulk, DIY: $15 in silicone, one Saturday.
  • Summer re-caulk, handyman: $75–$150 call-out plus $50–$200 per window.
  • One winter of neglected leak: $3,000 average for frame replacement, drywall repair, mold remediation, and repainting. Add tenant rent abatement if it went through HPD.
  • Full window replacement forced by rot: $300–$800 per window, and if it spread to three or four units, you're at $2,000–$3,000 fast.

The ratio is roughly 20:1 — a dollar spent in July saves twenty in March. That's before you count the tenant goodwill of a warm, dry apartment.

Windows aren't the only thing on this timeline, either. The same summer walk-through logic applies to checking your plumbing before August and walking your roof before fall. Do them the same weekend if you can — you're already up on the ladder.

Keep a Record

After your walk-through, log every window: apartment, condition, action taken, date. A photo per window on your phone is enough. Next June you'll know exactly which ones are due for another look and which ones are still holding — and if a tenant complaint or HPD inspection ever comes up, that record is the difference between a fast dismissal and a long argument.
