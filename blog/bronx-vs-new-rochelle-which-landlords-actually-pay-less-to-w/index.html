Bronx vs. New Rochelle: Which Landlords Actually Pay Less to Winterize Window Caulk and Weatherstripping Before January Tenant Complaints Spike?

Bronx landlords pay about 8–12% less than New Rochelle owners to re-caulk and weatherstrip windows before heat season — and a good chunk of the work you can safely do yourself in a weekend.

The short answer: Bronx wins, and part of it is a weekend DIY job

Bronx landlords pay less. A full re-caulk and weatherstrip on a typical 6–8 unit Bronx walk-up runs about $1,200–$2,400 with a licensed contractor, versus roughly $1,300–$2,700 in New Rochelle — Westchester trade rates sit 8–12% higher per hour. And the honest second half of the answer: the interior half of this job (sashes you can reach from inside, weatherstripping on apartment doors, kitchen and bath window frames) is genuinely owner-doable with a $12 tube of caulk and a utility knife. You only need a pro for the exterior envelope above ladder height.

If you've ever had a January call from a tenant on Grand Concourse saying their living room feels like a refrigerator, you already know this is cheaper to fix in September than to argue about in court.

Why does this get expensive if you wait?

In our experience managing Bronx buildings from Mott Haven to Riverdale, caulk fails on a predictable clock. Our winter swings — below 20°F some January nights, back up to 50°F by late February — push the window frame and the glass to expand and contract at different rates. Cheap latex caulk gets brittle and cracks in 3–5 years. You notice it as:

  • Visible hairline gaps where the bead has pulled away from the frame
  • Condensation (or outright frost) on the inside of the pane in early November
  • A clear cold draft when you hold the back of your hand near the sash on a windy day
  • Tenants quietly running space heaters, which spikes their ConEd bill and your complaint rate

Ignore it long enough and you're not just paying heat you can't hold — you're exposed under Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005, which requires weatherproofing against water infiltration and heat loss. A tenant HPD complaint during the October 1–May 31 heat season can land you a Class C violation and a $350–$1,000 fine on top of the repair.

Bronx vs. New Rochelle: what the numbers actually look like

Here's a side-by-side for the same hypothetical 6-unit building, roughly 24 windows, assuming full re-caulk plus new weatherstripping on sashes:

Line item Bronx New Rochelle
Labor rate (licensed caulker/glazier) $50–$85/hr $56–$95/hr
Service call / minimum $75–$150 $100–$175
Full job, 6–8 units $1,200–$2,400 $1,300–$2,700
Typical duration 1–2 days 1–2 days
Permit required for standard work No No

The Bronx advantage is simple supply: more trade shops within a 15-minute drive from Fordham Road or Hunts Point, so vendors compete harder on the service call fee. New Rochelle vendors often tack on a travel minimum you won't see here. The same pattern shows up in our breakdown of fall window and door sealing costs across the two markets.

What can you safely do yourself?

Honestly, more than most owners think. The interior side of a window — the part you can reach standing on an apartment floor — is not a licensed trade job. If you own the building and you're comfortable on a 2-step stool, you can do this on a Saturday.

What you'll need, about $60 total at any Bronx hardware store:

  • A tube of high-quality siliconized acrylic caulk (not pure latex — it hardens too fast in our climate). GE or DAP, around $9–$12.
  • A caulk gun with a smooth-rod trigger, $8–$15.
  • A 5-in-1 painter's tool or a stiff putty knife to scrape out the old bead, $10.
  • A roll of V-strip or foam weatherstripping for sashes and apartment entry doors, $12–$20.
  • Rags and rubbing alcohol to clean the frame before you re-caulk.

The actual technique: cut the old cracked caulk out in one strip, wipe the channel clean, cut your new tube's tip at a 45° angle no wider than the gap, and run one steady bead. Smooth it with a wet finger within 60 seconds. That's it. One apartment's worth of windows takes about 90 minutes once you've done the first one.

What should you NOT do yourself?

This is where owners get hurt — literally and financially. Do not touch:

  • Any exterior caulking above the second-floor windowsill. That's ladder work above 20 feet, and in NYC it needs licensed workers with proper fall protection. One slip from a 3rd-floor window and your homeowner's insurance will deny the claim.
  • Steel casement windows in prewar buildings (common in Grand Concourse Art Deco stock). The sashes need specialty glazing compound, not standard caulk.
  • Anything involving removing the sash or the window frame itself. That's a glazier call.

If you're weighing how much of this to hand off versus knock out yourself, our breakdown of when to replace weatherstripping vs. just re-caulk walks through the $300–$1,200 decision in detail.

When should this actually be done?

Aim to finish by October 15. That gives caulk 2–3 weeks to cure before the heat-on demands start November 1, and it gets you in front of contractors before their phones ring off the hook. Late August through early September is the sweet spot for booking — you'll get a vendor's "B" slot pricing instead of their "it's 28 degrees and my tenant is yelling" emergency rate.

After the job, whether you did it or hired it out, photograph every window — frame, bead, interior and exterior corners. Save the photos to a dated folder. That's what we use for our clients' annual compliance file, and it's exactly what you'll want in hand if a tenant files a §27-2005 complaint in February. So where does that leave you?

A Bronx owner who does the interior caulking themselves and hires out only the upper-floor exterior work on a 6-unit building usually spends $400–$900 total — materials plus a half-day pro visit. The same job fully contracted in New Rochelle lands at $1,300–$2,700. On a mid-size portfolio, that's real money, and more importantly, it's the difference between a quiet January and a stack of HPD complaints landing in your inbox the week of New Year's.
