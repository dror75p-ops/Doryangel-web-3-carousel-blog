Bronx vs. Mount Vernon: Which Landlords Pay Less for Windows & Weatherproofing Before Winter—and What $500–$1,500 in Sealing Actually Stops?

Bronx owners usually pay less for window sealing than Mount Vernon owners—but only if they book before Halloween. Here's what caulk and weatherstripping actually cost on both sides of the line, and the HPD violation a $500 tube-and-labor job quietly prevents.

If you've ever stood in a Bronx apartment in January and felt the curtain move while the window was shut, you already know what this post is about. The short answer: a 20-window Bronx walk-up will typically run you $500–$1,500 all-in for caulking and weatherstripping if you book in September or early October, versus roughly $700–$1,800 in Mount Vernon for the same scope — mostly because Westchester handyperson rates run $10–$20/hour higher and travel time gets added back in. And that $500–$1,500 is the difference between a quiet winter and an HPD Class C violation under Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005 for windows that aren't weather-tight.

Let's break down what you're actually paying for, what you can do yourself, and when to stop and call someone licensed.

What Does Window Sealing Actually Cost in the Bronx vs. Mount Vernon?

In our experience booking fall weatherproofing across both markets, here's what the numbers really look like in 2026:

Scope Bronx Mount Vernon
Handyperson labor rate $45–$65/hr $55–$80/hr
Inspection / call-out fee $75–$125 $100–$175
4–6 window seal job (labor) $400–$800 $500–$950

Bronx wins on raw rate, but Mount Vernon owners get one thing back: slightly easier parking, which shaves minutes off the clock. For most owners the Bronx number is still $200–$600 lower on a typical building.

What Is Caulk Actually Doing Up There?

Here's the plain-English version, because nobody explains this before handing you an invoice.

Caulk is the flexible rubbery bead you see where the window frame meets the brick or plaster. Its only job is to keep air and water from sneaking through that seam. Weatherstripping is different — that's the foam, felt, or rubber strip stuck to the moving parts of the window and door, so when you close them they press against something soft and seal.

Both fail the same way: sun beats on them, water freezes and thaws in the crack, and the material shrinks, hardens, and pulls away from the frame. In the Bronx you typically get 7–10 years out of exterior caulk before it starts letting go. The early signs are easy to spot once you know what you're looking at:

  • Visible hairline gaps along the perimeter of the frame
  • Brown water stains on the interior wall below the sill
  • A draft you can feel with the back of your hand on a windy day
  • Fog or condensation showing up between the panes (that one means the glass seal is gone, not the caulk — different fix)
  • Paint bubbling on the plaster around the window

If you're seeing two or more of these on the same window, that window is already costing you heat.

Can You Do This Yourself, or Do You Need a Licensed Contractor?

Good news here. Re-caulking around existing windows and installing weatherstripping do not require a DOB permit in New York City. These are maintenance tasks the owner is allowed to do with a $9 tube of exterior-grade silicone and a $4 caulk gun from the hardware store on Fordham Road.

Where you must stop and hire a licensed General Contractor with a DOB permit:

  • Replacing the window itself (the sash, the frame, or the glass unit)
  • Any facade work — if the brick around the window is cracked or the lintel above it is rusting, that's Local Law 11 territory and you do not touch it
  • Structural repair around the opening

Doing permit work without a permit triggers HPD violations and fines in the $250–$2,000 range per occurrence, and your insurance carrier will walk away from any related claim. Not worth it.

The DIY Caulk Job, Explained Like a Neighbor Would

If you're handling four or five windows in your own unit, here's roughly what you're doing:

  1. Pull the old caulk out with a 5-in-1 scraper or a plastic putty knife. It usually comes out in strips.
  2. Wipe the seam clean with a rag and a little rubbing alcohol — new caulk will not stick to dust or old silicone.
  3. Run a fresh bead of exterior-grade silicone or polyurethane sealant along the seam. Not latex — latex cracks in a Bronx winter.
  4. Smooth the bead with a wet fingertip or a $2 caulk-smoothing tool.
  5. Let it cure 24 hours before rain is forecast.

One window takes about 20 minutes once you've done two. A whole 2-bedroom apartment is a Saturday morning.

When Should You Actually Book This Work?

New York's heat season runs October 1 through May 31, which means the window to do this comfortably is tight:

  • September to mid-October: ideal. Dry, warm enough for caulk to cure, contractors not yet slammed.
  • April to May: second-best. The thaw exposes every seam that failed over the winter, so you can see exactly what needs redoing.
  • December to February: avoid. Caulk doesn't cure properly below about 40°F, contractors are chasing boiler emergencies, and you'll pay a rush premium.

The same seasonal logic applies to the fall roof and gutter work you should be bundling with this — one vendor, one trip, lower total cost. And if you're already walking the building, pair it with the downspout flush while the ladder is out.

What Does $500–$1,500 in Sealing Actually Stop?

This is the part owners underestimate. That modest spend prevents:

  • One HPD Class C violation under §27-2005 for windows not weather-tight — issued the first time a tenant calls 311 about a draft or water stain
  • Interior plaster damage from water tracking down the inside of the wall — a repair that starts at $400 per window and goes up fast if the drywall is soaked
  • A roughly 15–25% heat loss through unsealed windows, which on a landlord-paid-heat building in the Bronx is real money every month
  • The tenant-relations hit when someone is cold all winter and decides not to renew — turnover is the single most expensive thing that can happen to a rental unit

A 2–4 hour audit of a 20-unit building, with photos of every frame and notes on caulk condition, costs you an afternoon and saves the argument later about whether the damage was there at move-in.
